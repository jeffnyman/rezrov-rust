My goal with Rezrov is creating a working emulator of the Z-Machine and an interpreter for zcode programs that will run on that machine. Rezrov also takes aim at Glulx, a second and far roomier virtual machine built two decades after the first. Either way, what I am building is an interactive fiction interpreter. Depending on your age and/or nostalgia factor, you might better know "interactive fiction" by the name "text adventure."

Fun fact for nostalgia retrogamers: the term "interactive fiction" was likely first used by Robert Lafore.

The Z-Machine was originally created by a company called Infocom back in the late 1970s to play their adventure game Zork on microcomputers (which were the first "personal computers" with a side evolution into "home computers"). Eventually, this Z-Machine was used to play many text adventure games created by the same company throughout the 1980s.

Starting in the 1990s, after Infocom was acquired by Activision and effectively dissolved, hobbyists reverse-engineered the Z-Machine and games have been created for it ever since.

Those hobbyists eventually hit the same wall Infocom would have hit. The Z-Machine is a 16-bit design from the late 1970s, and by the 1990s the size of a story was being decided by the architecture rather than by the author. In 1999, Andrew Plotkin answered that with Glulx, a 32-bit machine designed for the same purpose with vastly more room to work in.

Neither machine replaced the other. Both are still in use, and the same authoring tools can compile to either one. So Rezrov aims at both: the six versions of the Z-Machine that Infocom built, the two later versions the Inform community added, and Glulx.

These "games" are often referred to as "stories," keeping with the focus on interactive fiction.
